How to Finance a Pool Route Purchase: Complete Guide

By Poolside Business Brokers · 14 min read

A typical pool route sells for 10 to 12 times monthly service revenue, which puts most listings in the $80,000 to $400,000 range. Few buyers have that sitting in a checking account — and even those who do often shouldn't tie it all up in one acquisition. This guide walks through every realistic financing option for a pool route purchase, what terms to expect, and what lenders actually care about.

The right financing structure depends on three things: how much cash you have available, your credit and business history, and how the seller is willing to structure the deal. We see most successful transactions use a blend of two or three sources rather than a single one.

Why Pool Routes Are a Lender-Friendly Asset

Before we get into specific options, it helps to understand why pool routes are financeable in the first place. Compared to many small businesses, pool routes have characteristics lenders like:

  • Recurring monthly revenue — customers pay the same amount every month, which makes cash flow predictable
  • Sticky customer base — well-run routes hold customers for 5+ years, so revenue projections are credible
  • Healthy margins — net profit on well-run owner-operated routes runs 50-55%, leaving plenty of room to service debt
  • Tangible underlying business — trucks, equipment, and a documented customer list provide some collateral value

That said, lenders can be cautious about service-based businesses because the "asset" is largely a customer list and recurring contracts. You'll often need to educate a generalist lender about pool route economics, or work with a broker who can connect you with lenders already familiar with the industry.

Option 1: All-Cash Purchase

Paying cash is the simplest path. You write a check, escrow releases the funds, and the route is yours. No interest, no monthly debt service, no underwriting timeline.

Pros

  • Strongest possible offer — sellers prefer cash because it closes fastest and eliminates financing contingencies
  • Often gives you negotiating leverage on price (we see all-cash buyers close 2-5% below comparable financed offers)
  • No monthly debt payment, so 100% of net margin is yours from day one
  • Faster close — typically 4 weeks instead of 8-12

Cons

  • Depletes working capital you may need for trucks, equipment, payroll, or unexpected repairs
  • No leverage means slower portfolio growth — the same $200,000 could be a down payment on a $400,000 route
  • Tax efficiency is worse — debt service creates a deductible interest expense; cash purchases don't

When It Makes Sense

All-cash works best when you're buying a smaller route ($80,000-$150,000) and have at least 12 months of personal living expenses plus operating reserves left over after the purchase. If buying the route would leave you with less than three months of expenses, finance some of it — the interest is cheaper than the risk of being undercapitalized.

Option 2: Seller Financing

Seller financing is when the seller agrees to receive part of the purchase price over time, with you making monthly payments to them directly. It's extremely common in pool route transactions — we'd estimate 40-50% of the deals we broker include some form of seller financing.

Typical Terms

  • Down payment: 50-70% at closing
  • Note duration: 12-36 months for the remainder
  • Interest rate: 6-9%, depending on relationship and market rates
  • Security: a promissory note, often with the route accounts pledged as collateral
  • Retention clauses: reductions in the note balance if accounts cancel within a defined window (often the first 60-90 days)

Pros

  • Easier to qualify for — the seller is your "lender" and decides whether your offer is acceptable
  • Faster than bank financing — no underwriting committee or 60-day approval cycle
  • Aligns incentives — the seller has an ongoing interest in your success and is usually more available for post-close questions
  • The retention clauses provide real protection against accounts cancelling during the transition
  • Lower or no closing costs compared to bank financing

Cons

  • Sellers usually want a meaningful down payment (rarely below 50%)
  • Shorter amortization means higher monthly payments than a bank loan
  • Some sellers want the full purchase price at closing and won't entertain seller financing at all
  • If you default, the seller can claw back the route, which is a more aggressive remedy than most bank loans

When It Makes Sense

Seller financing is the right tool when you have a reasonable down payment but don't have the full purchase amount, can't wait for a 60-90 day SBA approval, and the seller is willing to play ball. It's particularly common with retiring operators who would rather collect interest income than pay capital gains on a single lump-sum transaction.

Option 3: SBA 7(a) Loan

The SBA 7(a) program is the U.S. Small Business Administration's flagship loan product. The SBA itself doesn't lend — they guarantee a portion of loans made by participating banks, which lets those banks offer better terms than they'd give an unsecured borrower. SBA 7(a) is the most common bank-financed path for pool route purchases.

Typical Terms

  • Loan amount: up to $5 million (most pool routes fall well below this)
  • Down payment: typically 10-20% (10% is the SBA minimum, but most lenders want 15-20% on service business acquisitions)
  • Term: up to 10 years for business acquisitions
  • Interest rate: prime + 2.25% to 4.75%, depending on loan size and bank
  • Guarantee fee: 2-3.75% of the guaranteed portion, often rolled into the loan
  • Personal guarantee: required from any owner with 20%+ ownership

Pros

  • Lowest down payment of any debt option — you can buy a $200,000 route with $20,000-$40,000 down
  • Long amortization keeps monthly payments manageable, often allowing the route to fully self-fund the debt service
  • Competitive interest rates compared to alternative lenders
  • The longer the term, the more cash flow stays in your pocket each month for reinvestment

Cons

  • Slow — expect 60-90 days from application to funding (sometimes longer)
  • Document-heavy — tax returns, personal financial statements, business plans, projections, and route documentation
  • Lenders often require 2+ years of related experience or strong management skills
  • Lien on personal residence is common if you have substantial home equity
  • Some lenders won't finance "intangible" purchases (routes are mostly goodwill, not equipment), so you'll need to find one familiar with the industry

What Lenders Actually Look For

If you're applying for an SBA 7(a) loan to buy a pool route, an underwriter will focus on:

  • Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) — can the route's cash flow cover the loan payment with cushion? Most lenders want 1.25x to 1.5x
  • Credit score — 680+ is the practical floor, 720+ gets the best rates
  • Liquid post-close reserves — they want to see you'll still have several months of personal and business expenses available after the down payment
  • Industry experience or transferable skills — prior pool service work, fleet management, or other route-based service businesses help
  • Seller's verifiable financials — the seller's tax returns and bank statements must support the revenue claims, or the deal won't fund

This last point is critical. We've seen otherwise solid deals fall apart at the underwriting stage because the seller's "books" didn't match deposits in their bank account. If you're going the SBA route, work with a broker who insists on clean seller documentation before listing.

Option 4: Equipment Loans

Equipment financing is a narrower tool. It only covers the physical assets that come with the route — typically the service truck, trailer, and equipment package — not the customer list itself. For most route purchases, the equipment value is a small fraction of the total price, so equipment financing is rarely the primary funding source.

Typical Terms

  • Loan amount: 80-100% of equipment fair-market value
  • Term: 3-7 years, matched to useful life of the equipment
  • Interest rate: 7-12%, depending on credit and equipment type
  • Down payment: often 0-10%
  • Collateral: the equipment itself, no personal guarantee on the goodwill portion of the route

When It Makes Sense

Equipment loans are most useful as a complement to other financing. If you're paying cash for the customer list and goodwill but want to finance a $35,000 truck-and-trailer package separately, an equipment loan keeps that capital intact for working expenses. It can also be used when the seller's truck is older and you want to upgrade immediately at closing.

Option 5: Alternative & Online Lenders

This category includes online business lenders, revenue-based financing, and merchant cash advances. They're the most expensive form of financing but the easiest to qualify for and the fastest to fund — sometimes within 48-72 hours.

Typical Terms

  • Loan amount: $25,000-$500,000
  • Term: 6-36 months (short)
  • Effective interest rate: 15-40%+ APR equivalent
  • Approval timeline: 2-7 days
  • Documentation: minimal — usually a few months of bank statements and basic personal info

Pros

  • Speed — if a deal needs to close in 30 days, this can be the only option
  • Looser credit requirements
  • Less paperwork

Cons

  • Expensive — interest costs can eat 10-30% of route profit
  • Short terms mean high monthly payments that strain cash flow
  • Some products use daily debits, which is brutal for a route business with monthly customer billing cycles

When It Makes Sense

Almost never as the primary source for a route purchase. The math rarely works once you account for the true cost of capital against a pool route's 50-55% net margin. Where alternative lenders can play a role is as a short bridge — for example, while you wait for SBA approval — with a plan to refinance into something cheaper within months.

Blending Sources: The Most Common Structure

Most real-world pool route purchases use a combination of sources. A typical $200,000 route purchase might look like:

  • Buyer cash: $50,000 (25%)
  • Seller note: $50,000 (25%) over 24 months at 7%
  • SBA 7(a): $100,000 (50%) over 10 years

This blend gives the buyer manageable monthly debt service, gives the seller a meaningful chunk of cash at closing plus interest income, and gives the bank a borrower with real skin in the game. It's also robust — if the SBA piece runs long, the seller note can be expanded as a bridge.

For routes under $150,000, we more often see 60-70% cash plus 30-40% seller financing — the deals are small enough that the SBA paperwork isn't worth it for either side.

What Lenders Will Want From the Seller

If you're financing the purchase with bank debt of any kind, the seller's documentation matters as much as yours. Expect the bank to request:

  • 2-3 years of business tax returns
  • 2-3 years of profit and loss statements
  • Current account list with customer-level revenue
  • Bank statements showing actual deposits
  • Equipment list with fair-market valuations
  • Existing service contracts (if any)
  • Documentation of any chemical or supply arrangements

Sellers who can produce this in a day or two have routes that fund quickly. Sellers who can't are often the reason deals collapse. Our due diligence checklist for buyers covers exactly what to ask for before you make an offer.

Cash Flow Reality Check: Can the Route Service the Debt?

Before agreeing to any financing package, run the numbers on whether the route can actually carry the payments. Here's a realistic example for a $200,000 route generating $18,000 in monthly service revenue:

  • Monthly revenue: $18,000
  • Chemical costs (~20%): $3,600
  • Vehicle costs (~14%): $2,520
  • Insurance, software, misc (~10%): $1,800
  • Owner-operator labor (your time): not counted as a cash cost
  • Net cash flow before debt service: ~$10,080

If you're servicing a $100,000 SBA loan at 11% over 10 years (~$1,380/month) plus a $50,000 seller note at 7% over 24 months (~$2,240/month), your total monthly debt service is roughly $3,620. That leaves about $6,460 of owner take-home before taxes — healthy, but tighter than it would have been all-cash.

Once the seller note is paid off in year 3, your monthly take-home jumps by $2,240 with no change in revenue. That's the leverage benefit. For more on these unit economics, see our breakdown of pool route profit margins.

Common Financing Mistakes to Avoid

  • Stretching too thin on the down payment — using your last $30,000 to qualify for SBA leaves you with no reserves for a broken truck or seasonal cash flow dip
  • Ignoring the post-close cash flow needs — customers don't pay on day one. Plan for at least 30-45 days of expenses before the first full revenue month
  • Skipping the seller note — even a small seller note ($20,000-$30,000) keeps the seller motivated during the transition. Buyers who refuse seller financing on principle often pay for it in lost accounts
  • Going with the first lender that says yes — SBA terms vary widely between banks. Get 2-3 term sheets before committing
  • Underestimating the timeline — SBA loans take 60-90 days. If your offer says "30-day close" and you need SBA, you'll either lose the deal or pay a premium for fast money

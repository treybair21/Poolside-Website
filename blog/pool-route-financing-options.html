How to Finance a Pool Route Purchase: Complete Guide

By Poolside Business Brokers · 14 min read

Buying a pool route is a proven path to business ownership — but unless you're paying all cash, you'll need to figure out how to finance the purchase. The good news: pool routes are one of the more lender-friendly small business acquisitions out there, with multiple financing paths available to qualified buyers.

This guide covers every major financing option for pool route purchases: cash, seller financing, SBA 7(a) loans, equipment loans, and alternative lenders. For each option, we'll walk through how it works, the pros and cons, typical terms, and what you'll need to qualify. By the end, you'll know exactly which path makes sense for your situation.

Why Pool Routes Are Financeable

Before diving into the options, it's worth understanding why pool routes are attractive to lenders and sellers offering financing. Unlike many small businesses, pool routes have several characteristics that make them low-risk from a financing standpoint:

  • Recurring, predictable revenue — monthly service contracts create consistent cash flow that's easy to underwrite
  • Strong profit margins — well-run owner-operated routes produce 50–55% net margins, leaving substantial room to service debt
  • Tangible collateral — equipment, vehicles, and chemical inventory provide real assets
  • Low customer churn — pool service is a recurring, largely non-discretionary expense for homeowners
  • Established valuation methodology — the 10–12x monthly revenue multiple gives lenders a clear framework for assessing collateral value

These characteristics mean that both institutional lenders and individual sellers are often willing to extend favorable financing to qualified buyers. Let's look at each option in detail.

Option 1: All-Cash Purchase

The simplest financing option is no financing at all — paying the full purchase price in cash at closing.

How It Works

You bring certified funds (wire transfer or cashier's check) to escrow, the transaction closes, and you own the route free and clear from day one. There's no debt service, no lender approval process, and no interest cost.

Pros

  • Fastest path to closing — no lender underwriting means you can close in weeks rather than months
  • Strongest negotiating position — sellers almost always prefer cash buyers
  • No interest expense, which immediately improves your net margin
  • No risk of financing falling through mid-transaction
  • Maximum flexibility — you can sell the route whenever you want without lender approval

Cons

  • Requires substantial liquid capital upfront
  • Opportunity cost — capital tied up in the route can't be deployed elsewhere
  • No leverage means lower return on equity compared to financed purchases

When It Makes Sense

All-cash works best for buyers who have significant liquid assets, want to move quickly on a specific route, or are acquiring a smaller route where the purchase price is manageable. For a route generating $8,000/month, a cash purchase at a 10x multiple means bringing $80,000 to closing — a meaningful but not impossible sum for many buyers.

Option 2: Seller Financing

Seller financing — also called owner financing — is the most common financing structure in pool route transactions. The seller acts as the lender, accepting a portion of the purchase price over time rather than all at closing.

How It Works

Instead of receiving 100% of the purchase price at closing, the seller agrees to receive a down payment (typically 70–80% of the purchase price) at close, with the remaining balance paid over a defined period — usually 12 to 36 months. The buyer makes monthly payments directly to the seller, often at a negotiated interest rate between 6% and 10%.

The seller typically retains a security interest in the accounts (or the business assets) until the note is paid in full, providing protection if the buyer defaults.

Typical Terms

  • Down payment: 70–80% of purchase price at closing
  • Financed balance: 20–30% of purchase price
  • Term: 12–36 months
  • Interest rate: 6–10% annually
  • Payment structure: monthly principal and interest

Pros

  • Easier to qualify for than bank financing — the seller evaluates you, not a bank underwriter
  • Faster to close than SBA or bank loans — no lengthy approval process
  • Flexible terms that can be negotiated directly with the seller
  • Seller has skin in the game and is motivated to ensure a smooth transition
  • Lower upfront capital requirement than all-cash

Cons

  • Not all sellers will agree to carry a note — many prefer all cash
  • Shorter terms (12–36 months) mean higher monthly payments than SBA loans
  • You're in a direct financial relationship with the seller post-closing, which can complicate disputes
  • Seller financing is typically a smaller portion of the purchase price than an SBA loan

When It Makes Sense

Seller financing is ideal when you have a solid down payment (70%+) but want to preserve capital for operating expenses, equipment, or a second acquisition. It's also the fastest path to closing when you've found the right route and don't want to wait on a bank.

Option 3: SBA 7(a) Loan

The SBA 7(a) loan program is the federal government's primary small business lending program, and it's one of the best financing tools available for pool route acquisitions. SBA loans are issued by approved private lenders (banks and credit unions) but are partially guaranteed by the Small Business Administration, which allows lenders to offer more favorable terms than conventional loans.

How It Works

You apply for an SBA 7(a) loan through an SBA-approved lender. The lender underwrites the loan using SBA guidelines, and if approved, the SBA guarantees up to 85% of loans under $150,000 and 75% of loans above that amount. This guarantee reduces the lender's risk and allows them to offer longer terms and lower down payments.

For pool route acquisitions, the SBA 7(a) can finance the full purchase price minus a down payment, equipment, working capital, and even transition costs — all in a single loan.

Typical Terms

  • Loan amount: Up to $5 million (pool routes typically fall well under $500K)
  • Down payment: 10–20% of total project cost
  • Term: Up to 10 years for business acquisitions
  • Interest rate: Prime rate + 2.75–3.5% (variable, tied to prime rate)
  • Guarantee fee: 0.5–3.5% of the guaranteed portion (waived for loans under $150K)

What Lenders Look For

SBA lenders evaluate pool route acquisitions on several criteria:

  • Personal credit score: Most SBA lenders want a minimum 650 FICO, preferably 680+
  • Relevant industry experience: Prior pool service, outdoor services, or small business ownership experience strengthens your application
  • Cash flow coverage: The route's projected income must cover debt service plus your living expenses — lenders typically want a 1.25x debt service coverage ratio
  • Down payment source: You'll need to demonstrate that your down payment comes from legitimate sources (savings, gift with a gift letter, equity in another property)
  • Business plan: A basic plan showing how you'll operate and grow the route
  • Route documentation: Revenue history, customer list, service agreements, and equipment inventory

Pros

  • Lowest down payment requirement (10–20%) of any financing option
  • Long repayment terms (up to 10 years) mean the lowest monthly payments
  • Can finance the full acquisition plus equipment and working capital
  • Competitive interest rates compared to conventional business loans
  • Preserves your liquid capital for operations and growth

Cons

  • Slowest path to closing — SBA underwriting typically takes 30–90 days
  • More documentation required than any other option
  • The route must demonstrate sufficient cash flow to qualify
  • Some SBA lenders are unfamiliar with pool route transactions and may require more hand-holding
  • Guarantee fees add to the total cost of borrowing
  • Variable rate means your payment can increase if interest rates rise

SBA Lender Selection Tips

Not all SBA lenders are equally experienced with service business acquisitions. Look for lenders with a track record in small business acquisitions, or ask your broker for a referral. Preferred SBA Lenders (PLP status) have delegated approval authority and can close loans faster than non-PLP lenders.

When It Makes Sense

SBA 7(a) financing is the best option when you're acquiring a larger route and want to maximize leverage, or when you need to preserve capital for equipment, a vehicle, or operating expenses. It's also ideal for first-time buyers who have strong credit and relevant experience but limited liquid assets.

For example, if you're buying a route with $20,000/month in revenue at a 10x multiple ($200,000 purchase price), an SBA loan at 10% down means bringing just $20,000 to the table versus $140,000–$160,000 for a seller-financed deal or $200,000 all cash.

Option 4: Equipment Loans and Lines of Credit

Equipment financing is a narrower tool than SBA loans, but it can play a supporting role in a pool route acquisition — particularly when you need to finance vehicles or service equipment separately from the route itself.

Equipment Loans

Equipment loans are secured by the specific equipment being purchased (a service truck, trailer, chemical dosing equipment, etc.). The equipment itself serves as collateral, which makes these loans easier to qualify for than unsecured business loans.

  • Typical terms: 2–7 years, depending on equipment type
  • Rates: 6–18% depending on creditworthiness and lender
  • Down payment: Often 0–20%
  • Approval timeline: 1–5 business days for straightforward applications

Business Line of Credit

A business line of credit gives you revolving access to capital up to a set limit. It can be used for working capital, equipment, or to supplement other financing. However, lines of credit are rarely used to fund the full purchase of a route — they're better suited for managing cash flow during the transition period or funding additional equipment after closing.

When Equipment Financing Makes Sense

If you're buying a route that doesn't include a vehicle (some sellers keep their truck), or if you need to upgrade equipment to handle the accounts efficiently, equipment financing can let you spread that cost over time without tapping your SBA loan or cash reserves.

Option 5: Alternative and Online Lenders

Over the past decade, a new category of online lenders has emerged offering faster, more flexible small business loans — at a higher cost. Companies like Ondeck, Fundbox, Bluevine, and Kabbage offer term loans and lines of credit with streamlined applications and quick approvals.

Typical Terms

  • Loan amounts: $5,000–$500,000
  • Terms: 3–36 months
  • Interest rates: 15–60%+ annualized (far more expensive than SBA)
  • Approval timeline: 24–72 hours
  • Requirements: 6+ months in business, minimum revenue thresholds, 600+ credit score

Pros

  • Very fast approvals — often same-day or next-day funding
  • Minimal documentation compared to SBA or bank loans
  • More flexible underwriting for borrowers with imperfect credit

Cons

  • Significantly higher cost of capital than SBA or seller financing
  • Short repayment terms create high monthly debt service burden
  • Can quickly erode the profit margins that make pool routes attractive
  • Most alternative lenders require the business to be operating for 6+ months — which creates a chicken-and-egg problem for first-time buyers

When It Makes Sense

Alternative lending should generally be a last resort for pool route acquisitions. The high interest rates and short terms can turn a profitable 50–55% margin route into a cash flow nightmare. If you're considering this path, run the numbers carefully to ensure debt service doesn't eat your operating profit.

The one scenario where alternative lending makes sense: supplementing a mostly-cash or seller-financed deal with a short-term bridge loan to cover working capital during the transition period, with a clear plan to pay it off within 6–12 months.

How to Choose the Right Financing Option

The right financing path depends on four factors: how much capital you have available, how fast you need to close, what the seller will accept, and the size of the route you're buying.

If You Have Strong Liquidity (60%+ of Purchase Price)

Start by offering cash or seller financing with a large down payment. These structures are preferred by most sellers, give you the strongest negotiating position, and allow for the fastest closing timelines. You'll get the route you want more reliably than buyers who need 90-day financing approvals.

If You're Acquiring a Larger Route ($150K+ Purchase Price)

SBA 7(a) financing deserves serious consideration. The lower down payment requirement and longer repayment terms free up capital for other purposes — additional equipment, a second acquisition, or simply peace of mind. The 30–90 day approval timeline is a real constraint, so start the process early and be transparent with the seller about your financing plan.

If You're a First-Time Buyer With Limited Capital

An SBA 7(a) loan combined with a strong business plan and solid personal credit is your best path. Many first-time buyers successfully acquire routes generating $10,000–$20,000/month in monthly revenue with SBA financing, bringing as little as $10,000–$20,000 to the table. The key is demonstrating relevant experience (even adjacent experience like landscaping, field service operations, or small business management) and showing the lender that the route cash flows comfortably.

If the Seller Won't Wait on Financing

Some sellers — particularly those who've been on the market a while — need to close quickly and won't wait 60–90 days for SBA approval. In these cases, having access to bridge financing or a larger cash position lets you compete with all-cash buyers. Alternatively, some buyers line up SBA pre-approval before they start looking at routes, which compresses the post-offer timeline significantly.

A Note on Combining Financing Sources

Many pool route acquisitions use a hybrid structure. A common example: 75% cash down, 25% seller-financed over 24 months. Another: 10% down, 80% SBA loan, 10% seller note. These structures can align the interests of buyer and seller while minimizing the buyer's out-of-pocket cash at closing.

What to Do Before Applying for Financing

Regardless of which financing option you pursue, take these steps before you apply:

  1. Pull your personal credit report — check for errors, resolve outstanding collections, and understand where your score stands
  2. Organize your personal financial statements — most lenders want 2 years of personal tax returns and a personal financial statement
  3. Document your liquidity — 2–3 months of bank statements showing your down payment source
  4. Prepare a resume or bio — highlight relevant operational experience, even if it's not pool-specific
  5. Understand the route's financials — request monthly revenue data, a customer list, and any service agreements from the seller; lenders will want to see this

The buyers who close fastest aren't necessarily the ones with the most money — they're the ones who showed up prepared. Having your documentation organized before you make an offer dramatically speeds up the financing process.

How to Finance a Pool Route Purchase: Complete Guide

By Poolside Business Brokers · 14 min read

Financing is often the biggest hurdle for first-time pool route buyers — and one of the most misunderstood parts of the transaction. The good news: pool routes are among the more financeable small businesses out there, with predictable recurring revenue, low overhead, and a track record that lenders can evaluate. The key is knowing which option fits your situation.

This guide covers every major financing path for pool route buyers: all-cash, seller financing, SBA 7(a) loans, equipment loans, and alternative lenders. For each, we cover how it works, typical terms, the pros and cons, and what you'll need to qualify. By the end, you'll know exactly which options are available to you and how to position yourself to get the best deal.

Why Pool Routes Are Financeable

Not all small businesses are easy to finance. A restaurant with volatile cash flow, high inventory, and a lease-dependent location can be a nightmare for lenders. Pool routes are different.

A well-run pool route has:

  • Predictable monthly revenue — recurring service contracts mean lenders can model cash flow with confidence
  • Low operating costs — chemicals run about 20% of revenue, vehicles 13-15%, leaving strong margins for debt service
  • No lease dependency — the business operates from a vehicle, so there's no landlord risk
  • Net profit margins of 50-55% for well-run owner-operated routes — well above what most lenders require to cover loan payments

That 50-55% net margin is the critical number. On a $10,000/month revenue route, a solo operator typically clears $5,000-$5,500/month after expenses. That leaves substantial room to service a loan, making pool routes attractive to the right lenders.

The challenge is that pool routes are a niche asset class. Most banks, credit unions, and online lenders don't understand them. You need to work with people who do — whether that's a seller willing to finance the deal or an SBA lender experienced with service businesses.

Option 1: All-Cash Purchase

How It Works

The buyer pays the full purchase price at closing with no debt. Simple, clean, and fast.

Typical Terms

No ongoing payments. Full ownership immediately. Sellers often prefer cash offers and may accept a slightly lower price in exchange for a clean, fast close.

Pros

  • No interest expense — maximizes your take-home from day one
  • Strongest negotiating position — sellers value certainty and speed
  • No lender requirements, no approval process, no delays
  • No risk of financing falling through after you've done due diligence

Cons

  • Ties up significant capital that could be deployed elsewhere
  • Limits the route size you can acquire
  • Opportunity cost if your cash could earn returns elsewhere

Best For

Buyers with liquidity who want to move quickly or who are buying smaller routes (under $75,000). Also ideal for experienced operators adding a second or third route who already understand the business.

Option 2: Seller Financing

How It Works

The seller acts as the lender, accepting a down payment at closing and financing the remainder of the purchase price. The buyer makes monthly payments directly to the seller, typically over 1 to 3 years, until the balance is paid off.

Example: A route priced at $120,000 closes with a $36,000 down payment (30%) and $84,000 financed by the seller at 7% over 24 months. Monthly payments of roughly $3,750 until paid in full.

Typical Terms

  • Down payment: 20-40% of the purchase price
  • Interest rate: 6-10% annually, negotiable
  • Term: 12-36 months is most common; some deals stretch to 48 months
  • Security: the accounts themselves typically serve as collateral; seller may retain a security interest until paid off

Pros

  • Faster and more flexible than institutional financing
  • Seller has skin in the game — they're motivated for you to succeed
  • Terms are negotiable and can be customized to fit your cash flow
  • No bank credit check or business history requirements
  • Can close in weeks rather than months

Cons

  • Not all sellers are willing to finance — many want all cash at closing
  • Shorter repayment terms mean higher monthly payments than an SBA loan
  • If the seller needs cash urgently, this option may not be available
  • Requires trust between buyer and seller; a properly structured note and security agreement is essential

What Sellers Look For

Sellers who consider financing want to know: Can this buyer actually run the route? Do they have relevant experience, the right equipment, insurance, and licensing? And do they have enough cash reserves that one bad month won't cause a default? Demonstrating operational readiness is as important as your credit score in a seller-financed deal.

Best For

Buyers who are strong operators but don't want to wait for SBA approval, or who are buying from a seller looking for a quick-close deal. Also works well for routes in the $50,000-$200,000 range where the numbers pencil out cleanly on a 2-3 year payback.

Option 3: SBA 7(a) Loan

How It Works

The Small Business Administration's 7(a) loan program guarantees a portion of loans made by approved lenders, which reduces lender risk and makes financing available for businesses that might not qualify for conventional loans. Pool routes are eligible businesses under SBA guidelines.

You work with an SBA-approved lender (bank, credit union, or CDFI), apply for the loan, and if approved, the lender funds the purchase with the SBA guarantee backing a portion of the risk. SBA loans are the most common institutional financing path for pool route acquisitions in the $100,000-$500,000 range.

Typical Terms

  • Down payment: 10-20% of the purchase price
  • Interest rate: variable, typically prime + 2.75% to prime + 3.75% (check current rates; SBA caps the spread lenders can charge)
  • Term: up to 10 years for business acquisitions without real estate
  • Loan size: up to $5 million, but most pool route deals are $75,000-$350,000
  • SBA guarantee fee: assessed on the guaranteed portion; varies by loan size and term

Pros

  • Lower down payment than seller financing or conventional loans
  • Longer repayment term = lower monthly payments = more cash flow for you
  • Competitive interest rates, especially compared to alternative lenders
  • Lenders are accustomed to service business acquisitions through this program

Cons

  • Approval process takes 30-90 days — longer than seller financing
  • Requires strong personal credit (typically 680+ FICO), tax returns, and financial documentation
  • SBA may require a personal guarantee and collateral (often your home equity if available)
  • Lender must understand pool route business models — not all SBA lenders do
  • More paperwork and documentation than other options

What Lenders Look For

SBA lenders evaluating a pool route acquisition will focus on several things:

  • Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR): they want the business to generate at least 1.25x the annual loan payment. A route generating $60,000/year net should comfortably service a $40,000/year debt obligation.
  • Your personal credit history: 680+ FICO is a soft floor for most SBA lenders
  • Seller's financials: 2-3 years of tax returns or profit-and-loss statements from the seller, ideally showing consistent or growing revenue
  • Your relevant experience: lenders prefer buyers with some business ownership or service industry background
  • Personal liquidity: having cash reserves beyond the down payment signals you can weather early challenges

One important nuance: because pool routes are account-based businesses rather than asset-heavy ones, some SBA lenders classify them as "intangible asset" acquisitions, which can affect how they structure the loan. Work with a lender who has closed pool route deals before.

Best For

Buyers acquiring routes priced above $100,000 who want to preserve cash, have good personal credit and financial history, and can tolerate a 60-90 day closing timeline. SBA financing is the gold standard for larger acquisitions where the numbers clearly support debt service.

Option 4: Equipment Loans

How It Works

Equipment financing is designed for purchasing physical assets — vehicles, trailers, chemical dosing systems, and service equipment. While you can't finance the customer accounts themselves with an equipment loan, some buyers use equipment financing to cover the tangible asset portion of a deal, then pair it with other financing for the route premium (accounts).

Typical Terms

  • Down payment: 0-20%; many lenders will finance the full equipment value
  • Interest rate: 5-15% depending on credit and lender
  • Term: matches useful life of the equipment, typically 3-7 years
  • Collateral: the equipment itself, so no personal assets at risk

Pros

  • Fast approval — often 1-5 business days
  • Equipment secures the loan, so personal credit requirements are lower
  • Preserves cash for the customer list premium portion of the deal

Cons

  • Only covers tangible assets, not the customer accounts (which are usually the majority of the purchase price)
  • Must be combined with another financing method to cover the full deal
  • Adding multiple loan types increases complexity

Best For

Buyers who have the cash for the customer list portion but need to finance a truck, trailer, or equipment package. Also useful for existing pool service operators expanding their capacity who need vehicles and equipment but already have the route paid for.

Option 5: Alternative and Online Lenders

How It Works

Online lenders and alternative financing platforms have proliferated in recent years, offering faster approvals and less paperwork than traditional banks. Products include term loans, lines of credit, and revenue-based financing.

Typical Terms

  • Down payment: varies widely; some require 0%, others 20-30%
  • Interest rate: 10-40%+ — significantly higher than SBA or seller financing
  • Term: 6 months to 5 years
  • Speed: can fund in as little as 24-72 hours

Pros

  • Fast — ideal if you need to move quickly on a deal
  • More flexible credit requirements than banks
  • Can sometimes fund deals that don't fit SBA or conventional boxes

Cons

  • Interest rates can be extremely high, significantly cutting into your margins
  • Shorter terms mean high monthly payments
  • Some lenders use daily or weekly payment structures that strain cash flow
  • Total cost of financing can exceed 30-50% of the loan amount over the term

Best For

Use this option sparingly and only when others aren't available. The math needs to work: if a route generates 50-55% net margins and you're paying 30% of revenue to service high-rate debt, your actual take-home collapses. Alternative lenders make sense as a bridge in very specific situations, not as a primary financing strategy.

What Lenders (and Sellers) Actually Look For

Whether you're pursuing an SBA loan or seller financing, there are universal factors that will determine whether you get financing and on what terms:

1. Personal Credit Score

For institutional financing, 680+ is a practical floor. Above 720, you'll get better rates and easier approvals. If your credit is below 650, seller financing or a co-borrower may be your most realistic path.

2. Cash Reserves

Lenders want to see that you have money left over after the down payment. A common benchmark is 3-6 months of operating expenses in reserve. For a pool route generating $10,000/month with $4,500 in monthly expenses, that's $13,500-$27,000 in reserve beyond your down payment.

3. Relevant Experience

You don't need to be a pool technician, but demonstrating that you understand the business model, have owned or managed a small business before, or have service industry experience significantly strengthens your application. This matters more for seller financing than SBA, but lenders notice it either way.

4. The Route's Track Record

Consistent or growing revenue over 2-3 years, low customer churn, and clean financial records make the acquisition easier to finance. Lenders are essentially underwriting the route's cash flow as much as your creditworthiness. A well-documented route with 3 years of stable revenue is dramatically easier to finance than a route with spotty records or recent customer losses.

5. Debt-to-Income Ratio

If you have significant personal debt — mortgage, car loans, student loans — lenders will evaluate whether your total debt obligations are manageable alongside the new loan. Pool routes generally service debt well given their margins, but existing high personal debt can complicate the picture.

Structuring the Deal When Financing Is Involved

A few deal structure considerations that matter when you're financing a pool route:

Account Retention Guarantees and Financing

Most pool route purchase agreements include a customer retention clause — if accounts cancel during the transition period, the purchase price adjusts downward. When you're financing, this creates a useful alignment: the seller is motivated to help with a smooth transition because their payout (especially on seller financing) depends on you keeping the accounts.

Escrow and Timing

Always use escrow. When financing is involved, the escrow process coordinates the lender funding, seller payoff, and account transfer in the right sequence. Never accept a deal where accounts transfer before your funding is confirmed. Our buyer's guide covers the full closing process in detail.

Route Size and Monthly Payments

One practical check before committing to a financing structure: run the numbers on monthly debt service vs. expected take-home. A solo operator can handle up to about 60 accounts. If you're buying a 45-account route at $10,000/month revenue, your estimated net is around $5,000-$5,500/month. Make sure your monthly loan payment leaves enough to live on and build reserves. If debt service is eating 50%+ of your net, revisit the deal structure or purchase price.

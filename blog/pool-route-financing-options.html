How to Finance a Pool Route Purchase: Complete Guide

By Poolside Business Brokers · 14 min read

Financing is the piece that turns "I'd like to buy a pool route" into a closed deal. Most buyers have a rough idea of what they want to spend, but far fewer understand their actual options — how each one works, what it costs, and what the lender (or seller) will want to see before saying yes.

This guide breaks down every realistic financing path for buying a pool route: all-cash, seller financing, SBA 7(a) loans, equipment loans, and alternative lenders. We'll cover the mechanics of each, typical terms, the pros and cons, and the specific things underwriters look at when evaluating a pool route acquisition.

If you're still figuring out what a route purchase would actually cost, start with our pool route valuation guide — it will help you understand the price ranges you're working with before you decide how to fund it.

What You're Financing (and Why It Matters)

Before diving into the financing options, it helps to understand exactly what you're buying. A pool route is a service business — you're acquiring recurring customer contracts, not physical assets. The purchase price is almost entirely based on monthly revenue: pool routes typically sell for 10 to 12 times monthly service revenue.

That means a route generating $8,000 per month will sell for roughly $80,000 to $96,000. A $15,000-per-month route might fetch $150,000 to $180,000. These numbers matter for financing because traditional lenders are used to collateralizing loans against hard assets (real estate, equipment, inventory). With a pool route, the primary collateral is the cash flow from customer relationships — which is more nuanced but very real.

Understanding this distinction will help you choose the right financing path and frame your deal in a way that makes sense to lenders.

Option 1: All-Cash Purchase

How It Works

The simplest structure: the buyer pays the full purchase price at closing, typically through escrow. The seller receives full payment on closing day and the transaction is complete.

When It Makes Sense

Cash purchases work best for buyers who have liquid capital available and want maximum speed and simplicity. If you're buying a smaller route — say, $50,000 to $80,000 — and have the funds set aside, cash removes all the complexity of underwriting, approval timelines, and monthly debt service.

Cash buyers are also more attractive to sellers. A cash offer with no financing contingency closes faster and carries less execution risk than a financed deal. In a competitive situation — where multiple buyers are interested in the same route — a strong cash offer can win even if it's slightly below a financed offer.

The Tradeoff

The downside is simple: deploying all your capital into one asset leaves you with less cushion for operational expenses, equipment purchases, or future acquisitions. Pool routes are profitable businesses — well-run owner-operated routes generate 50–55% net margins — and leveraging some of that future cash flow through financing can preserve your liquidity for other uses.

Typical Terms

  • Full purchase price paid at closing via escrow
  • No ongoing debt service
  • No lender approval process or timeline
  • Fastest path to close

Option 2: Seller Financing

How It Works

In a seller-financed deal, the seller acts as the lender. Instead of receiving the full purchase price at closing, the seller accepts a down payment and carries a note for the remainder. The buyer makes monthly payments directly to the seller over an agreed term — typically 3 to 5 years.

For example, on a $120,000 route, a buyer might put down $60,000 (50%) at closing, with the seller carrying the remaining $60,000 at 8% interest over 36 months. Monthly payments would be roughly $1,880.

Why Sellers Offer It

Sellers who offer financing often do so because it broadens the buyer pool, speeds up the sale, and can produce a higher net sale price when interest is factored in. Many sellers also appreciate the predictable monthly income, especially if they're retiring and want a steady stream of payments rather than a lump sum they'll need to reinvest.

Why Buyers Like It

Seller financing is faster and more flexible than any institutional lender. There's no bank underwriting, no credit score minimums, no SBA approval timeline. Terms are negotiated directly between buyer and seller, which means the structure can be tailored to what works for both parties.

It's also a signal of confidence. A seller who's willing to carry a note is effectively saying, "I believe this business will continue to perform." That aligned incentive can be reassuring for first-time buyers.

Typical Terms

  • Down payment: 30–60% of purchase price
  • Interest rate: 6–10% annually
  • Term: 2–5 years
  • Amortization: equal monthly payments over the term, or interest-only with a balloon
  • Security: seller typically holds a lien on the route accounts as collateral

What to Watch For

Make sure the seller-financed portion is documented with a formal promissory note and a security agreement that perfects the seller's lien. Both parties should have legal counsel review the documents. Beware of deals with very short terms (12–18 months) that require a large balloon payment — make sure you'll have the cash flow or refinancing path to handle that.

Option 3: SBA 7(a) Loan

How It Works

The SBA 7(a) loan program is the most common government-backed financing vehicle for small business acquisitions. The SBA doesn't lend directly — instead, it guarantees a portion of the loan (typically 75–85%), which reduces the lender's risk and allows banks to offer better terms than they otherwise would on a business acquisition loan.

For pool route acquisitions, SBA 7(a) loans are best suited for deals above $100,000 where the borrower has strong personal credit, some industry experience, and the business has clean, documented financials. The program is explicitly designed for business acquisitions and includes working capital provisions.

Typical Terms

  • Loan amount: up to $5 million (most pool route deals fall in the $75,000–$500,000 range)
  • Down payment: 10–30% of purchase price
  • Interest rate: prime rate + 2.25–2.75% (variable), or fixed rates through some lenders
  • Term: up to 10 years for business acquisitions
  • SBA guarantee fee: 2–3.5% of the guaranteed portion, rolled into the loan
  • Collateral: all available business and personal assets; SBA does not require full collateralization if assets are insufficient

What Lenders Look For

SBA lenders evaluate four primary factors for pool route acquisitions:

  1. Business cash flow — the route must demonstrate sufficient cash flow to service the debt. Lenders typically require a Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) of 1.25x or higher, meaning for every $1.00 of annual debt service, the business generates $1.25 in cash flow.
  2. Borrower creditworthiness — personal credit score of 680+ is generally required; 700+ is preferred. Lenders will review your full credit history, outstanding debts, and financial statements.
  3. Relevant experience — you don't need to be a certified pool technician, but lenders want to see business ownership experience, trade skills, or a credible plan for managing operations. Having a management background or prior service business experience helps significantly.
  4. Verified financials — the seller must provide at least 2 years of documented revenue, ideally through bank statements or tax returns. This is where messy seller records can kill a deal. If the seller has been running cash revenue off the books, SBA financing becomes very difficult.

The Application Process

SBA 7(a) applications are more involved than conventional loans. Expect to provide: personal financial statements, 3 years of personal tax returns, a business plan or acquisition memo, the purchase agreement, the seller's financial records, and a description of the collateral. The approval process typically takes 30 to 60 days from application to funding — sometimes longer with community banks that do SBA lending occasionally.

Working with a Preferred Lender Program (PLP) lender can cut this timeline significantly. PLP lenders have delegated authority to approve SBA loans without full SBA review, which can shorten the process to 2–3 weeks.

Pros and Cons

Pros: low down payment, long repayment terms that reduce monthly debt service, fully amortizing (no balloon payments), and the program is explicitly designed for acquisitions like this.

Cons: slower process, significant paperwork, personal guarantee required, SBA guarantee fee adds to loan cost, and approval is not guaranteed even with strong credentials.

Option 4: Equipment Financing

How It Works

Equipment loans or leases are secured by the physical assets being financed — in the pool service context, that means service vehicles, pool cleaning equipment, chemical inventory, and trailers. Equipment lenders are accustomed to approving deals quickly (often in 24–48 hours) with less documentation than SBA loans.

Where It Fits in a Pool Route Deal

Equipment financing alone can't fund a full route acquisition — most of the purchase price represents the intangible value of customer contracts, not physical assets. But it can cover the tangible portion of a deal: the truck and trailer, cleaning equipment, chemicals, and tools.

For example, if a $100,000 route includes $25,000 in equipment and vehicles, an equipment loan might cover that $25,000 component, reducing the cash or other financing you need by that amount. This works well in combination with seller financing or SBA loans to minimize your upfront cash requirement.

Typical Terms

  • Loan amount: tied to the appraised value of the equipment
  • Down payment: 10–20%
  • Interest rate: 6–15% depending on creditworthiness and lender
  • Term: 3–7 years, aligned with the expected useful life of the equipment
  • Collateral: the equipment itself (typically no personal guarantee required for established businesses)

Option 5: Alternative and Online Lenders

How They Work

Online lenders like Funding Circle, Bluevine, OnDeck, and others offer term loans and lines of credit with faster approvals and lighter documentation requirements than traditional banks. They underwrite primarily on revenue and cash flow rather than collateral, which can be useful for financing a pool route acquisition when speed matters or when traditional lenders are less accessible.

Typical Terms

  • Loan amount: $25,000–$500,000
  • Interest rate: 10–30%+ (significantly higher than SBA)
  • Term: 1–5 years
  • Approval time: 24–72 hours in many cases

When to Consider Them

Alternative lenders are best used in limited situations: you need to close quickly, you don't qualify for SBA, or you need a smaller bridge amount to supplement other financing. They are not ideal as a primary financing source for most route acquisitions because the interest rates significantly increase your cost of capital and eat into the route's profitability.

At the profit margins pool routes generate — 50–55% net on well-run routes — carrying 25% interest debt is manageable for a short period, but it's the kind of decision that should be made deliberately, not by default.

How Lenders Think About Pool Route Cash Flow

Regardless of which financing path you pursue, every lender will evaluate the route's ability to service debt. Here's how to present pool route financials in a way that supports your application:

Document Monthly Revenue Clearly

Lenders want to see a stable, predictable revenue stream. Bank statements showing consistent monthly deposits that match the seller's claimed revenue are the gold standard. If the seller has software records from Skimmer, PoolBrain, or a similar platform, export them — they provide detailed account-level detail that supports the revenue figures.

Normalize for Owner-Operator Costs

Many pool route owners run personal expenses through the business. When presenting financials to a lender, have the seller's accountant (or your own) prepare a Seller's Discretionary Earnings (SDE) add-back schedule. This shows true operating cash flow by adding back items like owner salary, personal vehicle use, and one-time expenses.

Show Gross Margin

Chemical costs run around 20% of revenue for most routes, and vehicle/gas costs add another 13–15%. Lenders should see that after direct costs, the gross margin is in the 65–67% range before overhead. That margin is what services the debt and pays the owner-operator.

Address Customer Concentration

If a single customer accounts for a disproportionate share of revenue, lenders may view that as a concentration risk. For a diversified route with 40–60 residential customers each paying $150–$250 per month, this is rarely an issue — no single customer represents more than a few percent of revenue.

Structuring the Deal to Make Financing Work

One of the most effective ways to close a financed deal is to combine financing sources. Common hybrid structures include:

  • SBA + Seller Note: Buyer puts 10% down, SBA finances 80%, seller carries a 10% subordinated note (allowed under SBA rules, with restrictions). This minimizes buyer's cash requirement while keeping debt service manageable.
  • Cash + Seller Finance: Buyer pays 50–60% cash at closing, seller carries the balance over 3 years. Simple, fast, no bank involved.
  • Equipment Loan + Cash: Equipment lender covers the hard asset value, buyer brings cash for the intangible portion (the customer contracts). Works well for asset-heavy route packages.

What Buyers Often Get Wrong About Pool Route Financing

  • Assuming any lender will do — not all lenders understand pool route acquisitions. Working with a lender who has funded service business acquisitions before will save time and reduce frustration. Community banks and credit unions with SBA preferred lender status are often the best fit.
  • Underestimating working capital needs — the purchase price is just the beginning. Budget for chemicals, equipment maintenance, insurance, and 60–90 days of operating costs as you ramp up. Factor this into your financing structure or cash reserve.
  • Waiting to start the financing conversation — if you're serious about buying, get pre-qualified or at least have initial conversations with lenders before you're in a live deal. Most pool routes receive offers within 2 weeks of listing, and a buyer who already has financing lined up has a real competitive advantage.
  • Ignoring seller financing out of habit — many buyers default to "I'll get a bank loan" without ever asking the seller if they'd consider carrying a note. Seller financing is often the fastest, lowest-friction path — always worth exploring.

Putting It Together: Which Financing Option Is Right for You?

Here's a quick decision framework based on your situation:

  • You have the cash and want simplicity: All-cash purchase. Fast, clean, competitive with sellers.
  • You want to preserve capital and the seller is open to it: Seller financing, 40–60% down, 3–5 year note. Fastest financed path.
  • You're buying a larger route and want long repayment terms: SBA 7(a) loan with a preferred lender. Best long-term cost of capital for qualified borrowers.
  • The deal has significant equipment value: Equipment loan for the hard assets, cash or seller financing for the rest.
  • You need a small bridge amount quickly: Alternative lender as a short-term supplement, with a plan to refinance or pay down quickly.
